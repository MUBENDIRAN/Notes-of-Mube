1. Number system, exponents, and foundations

Parity and the small primes

Even ± even is even. Even ± odd is odd. Odd ± odd is even. The primes below 20 are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, and 19. Two is the only even prime.

For two numbers, product = HCF × LCM. The notes find the HCF and LCM by dividing by primes.

Powers and the last digit

a⁰ = 1, and a⁻ⁿ = 1 / aⁿ. Last-digit questions only need the cycle of the units digit. Divide the exponent by the cycle length and use the remainder. A remainder of 0 means the last entry in the cycle.

Cycle lengthUnits digit and the repeating pattern

24 → 4, 6  ·  9 → 9, 1
10, 1, 5, and 6 never change
  • Last digit of 7²⁰². 202 ÷ 4 leaves remainder 2, so the second entry of 7, 9, 3, 1. The last digit is 9.
  • Last digit of 9⁹⁹. 99 ÷ 2 leaves remainder 1, so the first entry of 9, 1. The last digit is 9.
  • Last digit of 7¹⁰³ × 6²⁵ × 8⁴⁴. 103 leaves remainder 3, so 7’s cycle gives 3. 6 stays 6. 44 is divisible by 4, so 8’s cycle gives its fourth entry, 6. 3 × 6 × 6 = 108, so the last digit is 8.
  • 2⁵ × 2⁷ ÷ 2³ = 2¹² ÷ 2³ = 2⁹ = 512. Same base: add exponents to multiply, subtract to divide.

Near 100, and three multiplications

(100 + x)² is the number (100 + 2x), with x² written in the last two digits. (100 − x)² uses (100 − 2x) the same way. 98² is (100 − 4) followed by 04, which is 9604.

The notes split a cube near 100 into the binomial pieces (100 ± 3x), 3x², and x³. Those are parts of the expansion, and carries have to be applied. They are not three blocks you paste. 102³ = 1,000,000 + 3×10,000×2 + 3×100×4 + 8 = 1,061,208.

4. Profit and loss

SP is the selling price, CP the cost price, MP the marked price. Profit or loss is SP − CP. Positive is profit. Markup is on CP. Discount is on MP. Profit and loss percentages are always on CP, comparing CP with SP.

  • Profit% = [(SP − CP) / CP] × 100. Loss% = [(CP − SP) / CP] × 100.
  • SP = CP × (1 + profit%/100), or CP × (1 − loss%/100).
  • CP = SP × 100 / (100 + profit%), or SP × 100 / (100 − loss%).
  • A price that rises by a% and then falls by b% finishes at the start × (1 + a/100) × (1 − b/100). Successive discounts of a% and b% equal a single discount of a + b − (ab/100).

The reverse method is the one to practise: from SP and the gain, recover CP. SP = ₹600 at a 20% gain means CP = 600 × 100 / 120 = ₹500. The multipliers in the notes are +40% = ×1.40, +20% = ×1.20, −10% = ×0.90, and −25% = ×0.75.

5. Ratio, averages, and mixtures

If A : B = m : n and the total is T, then A is m/(m+n) of T and B is n/(m+n) of T. A 2 : 3 split of 500 is 200 and 300. The fourth proportional in a : b = c : d is d = (b × c) / a.

Average = sum / count. If the average changes, the new average is the old average plus (the change in the total) / (the new count). A symmetric run such as 48, 49, 50, 51, 52 has average 50, the middle value.

  • Five numbers average 8. Remove 10. The sum was 40, then 30, over 4 numbers: the new average is 7.5.
  • Seven numbers average 15. After adding x the average is 16. Old sum 105, new sum 128, so x = 23.
  • 20 boys averaging 60 and 30 girls averaging 70. Combined average = (1200 + 2100) / 50 = 66.

Alligation

To hit a mixture strength between two known strengths, the ratio of the amounts is the two differences from the target, crossed over. The strength farther from the target contributes the smaller share.

  • 30 L at 20% alcohol contains 6 L of alcohol. 40 L at 15% salt contains 6 L of salt.
  • 20 L at 25% alcohol. Remove 5 L and replace it with water. Alcohol left is 5 − 1.25 = 3.75 L, which is 18.75% of 20 L.
  • 10% and 30% to make 20% is 1 : 1. 20% and 50% to make 30% is 2 : 1 of the 20% and the 50%. 15% and 45% to make 30% is 1 : 1. 20% and 70% to make 50% is 2 : 3. 30% and 60% to make 45% is 1 : 1.
A target outside both strengths. The notes ask for 40% from 10% and 30%, and correctly say it cannot be done. Mixing two solutions cannot land outside the range between them. Ignore the 1 : 3 the absolute differences produce. That ratio is not a real mixture.

6. Simple and compound interest

Simple interest is SI = (P × R × T) / 100, and the amount is A = P + SI. Working backwards, P = A / (1 + RT/100). Time in a fraction of a year is a fraction in that formula: 9 months is 0.75 year.

7. Time and work, pipes, and mensuration

A person who finishes a job in x days does 1/x of it per day. Two people together do 1/x + 1/y per day, so the job takes xy / (x + y) days. Efficiency and days are reciprocals: more efficiency means fewer days.

A pipe that fills in x hours contributes +1/x. A leak that empties in y hours contributes −1/y. Add the signed rates.

  • A in 12 days and B in 8 days finish together in 4.8 days. A in 10 and B in 15 finish in 6 days.
  • A in 12 days works alone for 4 days, which is 1/3 of the job. B, who takes 18 days, does the remaining 2/3 in 12 days. Total 16 days.
  • A in 9 days works 3 days alone (1/3). Together A and B, who takes 12 days, do 7/36 per day, so the rest takes 24/7 days, about 3.43. The notes’ total is about 6.42 days.
  • A fill in 10 hours and a leak in 15 hours give a net 1/30 per hour, so 30 hours. Fillers of 12 h and 18 h with an emptying pipe of 36 h give 1/9 per hour, so 9 hours.

8. Time, speed, distance, trains, and clocks

Speed = distance / time. 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s, and 1 m/s = 18/5 km/h. If speed rises by x%, time falls by [x / (100 + x)] × 100 percent.

Opposite directions: add the speeds. Same direction: subtract them. A train passes a pole in (length of train) / speed. It passes a platform in (train + platform) / speed. Downstream speed is boat + stream. Upstream is boat − stream.

  • 60 km in 2 hours is 30 km/h. 54 km/h is 54 × 5/18 = 15 m/s.
  • Cars 210 km apart, closing at 60 and 50 km/h, meet in 210/110 ≈ 1.91 hours. The notes write 1.90.
  • A 150 m train at 54 km/h (15 m/s) passes a pole in 10 s.
  • A 180 m train at 72 km/h (20 m/s) passes a man walking the same way at 6 km/h (5/3 m/s). Relative speed is 55/3 m/s, and the time is about 9.82 s.
  • Trains of 120 m at 15 m/s and 180 m at 10 m/s, approaching: relative speed 25 m/s over 300 m, so 12 s.

Clocks

The minute hand moves 6° per minute. The hour hand moves 0.5° per minute, which is 30° per hour. At H hours and M minutes the hands are at 30H + 0.5M degrees and 6M degrees. The angle in the notes’ first example is the absolute difference. Take the smaller of that difference and 360° minus it when the question asks for the angle between the hands.

The hands overlap h hours after 12:00 at (60h) / 11 minutes past h. The notes’ shorthand “angle = |minutes − hour|” is not the formula. Use the degrees above.

  • At 3:20 the hour hand is at 3×30 + 20×0.5 = 100°, and the minute hand is at 120°. The difference is 20°.
  • A right angle between 3 and 4 satisfies |5.5t − 90| = 90. One solution is 3:00. The other is t = 180/5.5 ≈ 32.73 minutes, so about 3:32.73.
  • The overlap between 4 and 5 is (60 × 4) / 11 = 240/11 minutes, which is 4:21 and 9/11.

9. Permutation, combination, and probability

If one action can happen in A ways and a second, independent action in B ways, together they can happen in A × B ways. “At least one” is the total minus the “none” case.

Order matters for a permutation: nPr = n! / (n − r)!. A combination only selects: nCr = n! / [r! (n − r)!].

  • 3-letter words from A, B, C, D with no repeat: 4 × 3 × 2 = 24. Four-digit numbers from 1 to 5 with no repeat: 5P4 = 120.
  • Choose 3 from 8: 8C3 = 56. Committees of 4 from 7: 7C4 = 35.
  • A team of 3 from 3 boys and 2 girls, at least one girl: 5C3 − 3C3 = 9. The notes’ larger variant, 5 boys and 3 girls choosing 3 with at least one girl, is 8C3 − 5C3 = 46.
  • A is always on a team of 3 from 7: choose the other 2 from 6, so 15. A and B always together on a team of 3 from 6: 4 ways. Never together: 20 − 4 = 16.
  • 4 from 8 people, of whom 3 are girls, at least one girl: 8C4 − 5C4 = 65.

P(E) = favourable / total, and P(not E) = 1 − P(E), with the probability between 0 and 1. A deck has 52 cards, 4 suits of 13, and 4 each of ace, king, queen, and jack.

  • 5 red and 3 blue. P(one red) = 5/8. P(two red) = 5C2 / 8C2 = 5/14.
  • 4 red, 3 blue, 2 green. P(two of the same colour) = (6 + 3 + 1) / 36 = 5/18.
  • From that same bag, P(at least one red in three draws) = 1 − 5C3/9C3 = 37/42.
  • 5 red and 3 non-red, two drawn: P(at least one red) = 1 − 3/28 = 25/28.
  • Two cards: P(both aces) = 4C2 / 52C2 = 1/221. P(one ace and one king) = 16/1326 = 8/663.
Remember: nPr when the order is a different outcome, nCr when it is only a group. “At least one” is usually 1 minus the chance of none.

10. Venn diagrams and data interpretation

For two sets, “only A” is A minus the overlap. “At least one” is the total minus neither. Both = A + B − (at least one). “At most one” is neither, plus only A, plus only B.

Three sets have eight regions. Exactly one is the three “only” regions. Exactly two is the three pairwise-only regions. At least two is exactly two plus the centre. At most one is neither plus exactly one.

  • 50 students, 28 maths, 20 physics, 10 both. Only maths 18, only physics 10, at least one 38, neither 12.
  • 60 people, 35 tea, 30 coffee, 5 neither. At least one is 55, so both = 35 + 30 − 55 = 10.
  • 100 students, 60 cricket, 45 football, 25 cricket only. Both = 35, so football only = 10.
  • 100 people, 40 like A, 35 like B, 30 like C. The notes treat 10, 8, and 5 as the exactly-two regions, and 7 as all three. Only A, B, and C are then 15, 13, and 10. At least one is 68, so none is 32. Exactly two is 23, at least two is 30, exactly one is 38, at most one is 70, and exactly one or two is 61.
Read “A and B” carefully. In that last example the 10, 8, and 5 are the pairwise-only counts, not the full intersections. If a question’s “10 like A and B” already includes the people who like all three, subtract that centre before you compute “only A”.

Reading a table

Read the question before you total every column. Growth percent is (new − old) / old × 100.

11. Reasoning, data sufficiency, and a little algebra of logic

Blood relations

Name the person the phrase is about, then step one relation at a time. The notes’ chain is: Ravi is Suresh’s father; M is N’s brother; A is B’s sister and B is C’s father, so A is C’s aunt or uncle; P is Q’s father, Q is R’s sister, and R is S’s mother, so S is P’s grandchild; X is Y’s brother and Y is Z’s mother, so X is Z’s maternal uncle; A is B’s father, C is B’s sister, and D is C’s daughter, so D is A’s granddaughter; A and B are siblings, C is A’s daughter, and D is B’s son, so C and D are first cousins.

Directions, calendars, and seats

Turning right cycles north, east, south, west. Turning left cycles north, west, south, east. Facing north, a right turn faces east. The notes’ longer walk — face south, then left, left, right, left — ends facing north.

A normal year shifts the calendar by one odd day. A leap year shifts it by two. A year is a leap year when it is divisible by 4; a century year also has to be divisible by 400. In a row, the total count when someone gives “left” and “right” neighbours is left + right − 1, because that person was counted twice.

Four people in a line can sit in 4! = 24 orders. Fix one person at the left end and the rest have 3! = 6 orders. Two particular people sitting together can be treated as one block: 3! × 2 = 12, and the arrangements where they are apart are the other 12.

The notes also sketch one fixed row of six facing north and one circle of eight facing the centre. Those depend on the option list in the PDF. The useful habit is the same: place the person who is fixed, then the “immediate” neighbour, then the person opposite, and cross off seats that the negative clues forbid.

One truth-teller among P, Q, and R: P says Q is guilty, Q says R is guilty, R says Q is innocent. Exactly one statement is true. If Q is the truth-teller, then R is guilty, P is false, and R is false. That is consistent. The notes’ other branch, taking P as the only truth, makes Q guilty instead. Check which statement is the single true one before you lock the guilty person.

Data sufficiency

The option letters in the notes are the usual ones. A: statement I alone. B: II alone. C: both together, and neither alone. D: either statement alone. E: even together, not enough. Test I by itself, then II by itself, and combine only if each one fails. A yes/no question needs one definite answer, not a number.

Syllogisms and logarithms

“All A are B” does imply “some B are A”, as long as A is not empty. “Some A are B” does not imply “all A are B”. “No A are B” is a negative: it contradicts “some A are B”.

For positive a and b, and a valid base: log(ab) = log a + log b, log(a/b) = log a − log b, and log(aⁿ) = n log a.
